# Literacy cover note: supported and unsupported claims

**Length:** 45 minutes  
**Learning outcome:** Learners distinguish a claim that is supported by the information in a paragraph from a claim that is unsupported or overstated, and explain how the evidence does or does not match the exact claim.

## Materials and setup

- Paper and pencils.
- The two paragraphs printed or copied from this note.
- Blue tray for the completed work.
- No classroom account or display slides are needed. Reading the paragraphs aloud is fine.

If the paragraphs cannot be copied or displayed, read each paragraph aloud twice. Learners can write `A` and `B`, note the claim and the relevant information in their own words, and complete the same explanation. This keeps the reasoning task intact.

## Learner-facing wording

Read this aloud or write it on paper for the class:

> Today we will decide whether a claim is supported by the evidence in a paragraph. A claim is something the writer wants us to accept. Evidence is information that bears directly on that claim. Mark the claims in Paragraphs A and B, underline or circle information that supports them, and mark any evidence that is too weak or does not match the claim. Then write one sentence explaining why one claim is supported or unsupported. Use only the information in the paragraphs. Put your marked-up work and sentence in the blue tray at the end.

### Paragraph A

> Every class should start with silent reading because it makes every learner improve. Last Friday I enjoyed our silent reading time.

### Paragraph B

> Our group found the printed directions easier to use than the audio directions in yesterday's task. Four of our five members completed the task with the printed directions; two completed it with the audio directions. The tasks were different, so this does not establish that print is always better.

## Timed sequence

| Time | Action and facilitator cue |
|---|---|
| 0–4 min | Settle learners, provide paper and pencils, read the learner-facing wording, and point out the blue tray. Say that slides and account access are not needed. |
| 4–8 min | **Diagnostic:** Read the first sentence of Paragraph A. Ask: “What does the writer want us to accept?” and “Which sentence is only a personal experience?” Learners mark a `C` beside a claim and a brief `P` beside the personal experience. If several learners cannot separate these, spend three minutes restating “what the writer wants us to accept” and model one contrast before continuing. If they can, move directly to the model. |
| 8–15 min | **Model Paragraph A:** Think aloud while learners follow. Treat “Every class should start with silent reading” and the broad “every learner improve” assertion as the main claim. Show the plausible wrong answer: “Last Friday I enjoyed our silent reading time” is evidence that every learner improves. Ask: “Does this tell us about every learner’s improvement, or only one person’s experience?” Make clear that enjoyment is not evidence for the universal claim. |
| 15–22 min | **Guided Paragraph B:** Learners mark the claim, the two counts, and the limiting sentence. Ask: “What do the counts support, if anything?” and “Are these results from the same task?” Accept the limited conclusion that this group reported different completion counts; do not accept “print is always better,” because the tasks were different. |
| 22–30 min | **Supported practice:** Learners annotate both paragraphs. Offer this four-question scaffold: (1) What exact claim is being made? (2) What information bears on it? (3) Does that information match the claim’s scope, such as “every” or “always”? (4) What can the paragraph safely conclude? Let learners discuss quietly if helpful, but keep each learner’s final marking their own. |
| 30–39 min | **Independent application:** Each learner completes the markings and writes one sentence about one supported or unsupported claim. A useful optional frame is: “The claim that ___ is [supported/not supported] because ___; this [does/does not] match the exact claim because ___.” Remove the frame for learners who do not need it. |
| 39–45 min | Ask one final oral check: “What is the danger in changing ‘this group on this task’ into ‘always’?” Collect each learner’s marked-up work for A and B and one-sentence explanation in the blue tray. Complete the staff record below. Protect this collection time if earlier discussion runs long. |

## How to judge the collected work

Use an observable checklist for this exercise, not an official grade scale:

1. **Claim marking:** The learner marks the main claim or claims in each paragraph. A reasonable marking may treat “Last Friday I enjoyed our silent reading time” as a personal claim or observation, provided it is not treated as proof of the universal claim.
2. **Paragraph A evidence link:** The learner recognizes that one person’s enjoyment is not evidence that every learner improves or that every class should start with silent reading.
3. **Paragraph B evidence limit:** The learner recognizes that the counts describe this group’s results but that the tasks were different, so they do not establish that print is always better.
4. **Explanation:** The one sentence names a claim and explains whether the information supports the exact claim, including its scope. “Four is more than two, so print is always better” shows the target misconception; “the tasks differed, so the result is limited” is a valid explanation.

Do not infer long-term progress, effort, motivation, disability, or support-plan status from this single exercise. If a learner’s markings are incomplete, record the observable gap rather than a broader judgment.
