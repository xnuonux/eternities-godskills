# Scene continuity and coverage

Use for storyboards, animatics, shot lists, filmed or generated scenes, and
continuity repairs. Deliver the requested creative artifact, not an obligatory
production system. A single image or minor caption edit usually does not need
this method.

## Establish what the scene does

Identify the change in the scene: who wants what, what interferes, what action
makes the change visible, and what the audience should know at each moment.
Distinguish supplied story facts and locked decisions from your creative choices.
Use reasonable labeled assumptions for noncritical gaps; ask only when a missing
choice would materially redirect the work.

Decide whether the task is new direction, translation of a settled shot into
prompts, or a local repair. New direction may invent within the user's brief.
Prompt translation preserves settled action and camera decisions; a continuity
repair does not license rewriting the whole scene.

## Build one world and one clock

Keep a minimal scene map: landmarks, entrances, character positions and facing,
prop ownership, meaningful light sources, and action order. Add detail when it
affects a shot. Do not require a particular folder layout or companion agent.

Place story events on a shared scene timeline. Alternative camera angles cover
intervals of the same action; they do not each add new story time. A cut to a
close-up of a handoff should not restart the handoff unless a replay is intended.
Track the state immediately before and after each event and each selected cut.

For each shot, decide the visible information and emotional emphasis first, then
choose framing, viewpoint, depth, motion, focus and duration. Preserve expressive
ambition: complex motion is allowed when its path and purpose are coherent.
Simplify only for an observed limitation or a deliberate creative choice.

## Define camera coverage that can be checked

Each useful shot description establishes:

- the scene interval or event being covered;
- the starting composition, subject position and spatial orientation;
- the action, camera movement and focus changes that reveal information;
- the ending composition and state passed to the next shot;
- the sound or dialogue whose timing affects the cut.

Specify camera translation separately from turning or changing focal length.
Recompute screen direction, eyelines, occlusion and lighting from the camera's
view of the same world. Intentional disorientation or axis crossing is valid;
make the transition legible instead of treating a convention as an absolute ban.

Use references according to what they actually establish: appearance, location,
composition, timing or movement. A still image does not prove an intervening
motion, and a rough previs does not lock final art direction.

## Review in time, then revise locally

Check event order, entrances, contacts, prop transfers, clothing, geography,
light direction and audio timing across cuts. Inspect motion or temporal samples
when available; matching endpoints alone cannot establish continuity. Test the
sequence at its intended aspect ratio and playback speed.

Separate the world-time problem from the edit-time problem. Change a cut or
choose another angle when coverage suffices; regenerate or re-shoot only the
interval with a demonstrated gap. Preserve accepted takes and record which
version the edit actually uses. Provider duration limits and supported controls
must be checked against the tool in use, not assumed from a generic template.

Deliver a readable shot sequence, prompts or animatic as requested, with only the
continuity notes needed for execution. No fixed panel count, image model, grid,
project directory, or mandatory handoff is part of this skill. Generated prompts
are not generated media; a saved animatic is not proof of a successful final shot.

## Example

A character crosses a room during seconds 0-4, gives a letter away during 4-6,
then reacts during 6-8. The wide take covers 0-8; a hand close-up covers 4-6.
The edit can use wide 0-4, close-up 4-6, wide 6-8 and remain eight seconds long.
Appending the close-up after the whole wide take would repeat the event instead.
Before accepting the cut, verify which hand holds the letter, the receiver's
position, the established light, and the reaction's timing.
